# Lean and Agile Book Repositioning Design

## Goal

Reposition the book from a methodology-comparison manuscript into a stronger, more marketable argument:

1. enduring principles still govern good work,
2. AI has shifted the bottleneck in delivery,
3. classic methods still matter, but some of their assumptions and rituals no longer fit,
4. the second half of the book should introduce and defend a new AI-era operating methodology without pretending it arrived from nowhere.

## Why This Pivot Is Necessary

The current manuscript has a real voice and a usable first half, but its strategic center is no longer strong enough.

The old center was:

> Lean, Agile, Six Sigma, ITIL, and related methods are more alike than people think.

That idea is true, but it is not yet distinctive enough to carry a strong trade book in 2026.

The stronger center is:

> AI has not changed the underlying truths of quality, trust, learning, governance, and leadership. It has changed where work gets stuck. The bottleneck is shifting from code production to context coordination, decision quality, governance, integration, and adoption.

That shift creates room for a stronger book:

- more timely
- more marketable
- more practical
- more defensible

## Revised Core Thesis

Most improvement methodologies still rest on the same enduring principles, but AI has changed the shape of delivery work enough that organizations need a new operating model for how intent, context, judgment, execution, and accountability move through the system.

Plain version:

> The truths are old. The bottleneck is new.

## What Stays

These elements remain central:

- Deming and systems thinking
- quality is built, not inspected
- fear kills improvement
- leaders remove barriers
- employees closest to the work hold vital insight
- process and culture are inseparable
- AI should augment, not diminish
- wit, warmth, and practical realism

The tone does not need to become colder, trendier, or more technical. The manuscript still needs the same human center.

## What Changes

### The book's argument changes

The book should no longer stop at "these frameworks are all related."

It should now argue:

- what those frameworks were trying to solve
- which problems they still solve well
- where AI exposes new failure modes
- what a better AI-era operating method looks like

### The book's back half changes

The later chapters should stop behaving like short concluding glosses and start functioning as the proving ground for the new operating model.

### The title can change

The current title may become too backward-looking once the new method becomes central.

That does not require an immediate title decision, but it means title exploration is now strategic rather than cosmetic.

## Scope And Audience

This should remain an IT-centered book with broader business relevance.

That means:

- the center of gravity stays in software, IT, systems, delivery, and digital operations
- the lessons should clearly apply to business analysis, project governance, change management, and adjacent business functions
- the book should not pretend to be equally native to every domain

The right positioning is:

> a software-and-delivery book that explains something larger about modern organizational work

That is stronger than making it a vague "all business" book.

## Framework Map

The new methodology should be framed as a synthesis, not a rebellion.

### Deming / Lean

Still contribute:

- systems thinking
- waste reduction
- quality at the source
- management responsibility
- flow
- respect for workers

### Agile / Scrum

Still contribute:

- learning through short cycles
- adaptability
- iterative delivery
- tighter feedback loops

But some assumptions are now under pressure:

- human code production as a dominant delivery constraint
- human-only execution capacity
- role designs that assume coordination primarily among people rather than across people and agents
- ceremonies that become bureaucracy if not reconsidered

Important rule:

Do not say Agile solved only the code-writing bottleneck. That is too simplistic and will weaken the book.

Better claim:

Agile solved real problems, but AI changes the economics and rhythm of execution enough that some Agile rituals and role assumptions deserve revision.

### ITIL

Still contributes:

- service stability
- change discipline
- incident thinking
- explicit operational governance
- value of predictable control in chaotic environments

This likely becomes more important in an AI-agent world, not less.

### PMBOK / PMI

Useful carry-forward ideas:

- tailoring beats dogma
- governance matters
- value delivery matters more than artifact completion
- predictive, agile, and hybrid are choices, not religions
- modern project management is broader than scheduling tasks

PMI is currently positioning PMBOK around principles, performance domains, tailoring, value delivery, and emerging topics including AI.

### BABOK / IIBA

Useful carry-forward ideas:

- business analysis is about enabling change by defining needs and recommending solutions that deliver value within a context
- stakeholder engagement is real work
- governance and information management matter
- requirements architecture matters
- solution recommendation is distinct from implementation

This is critical for broadening the book beyond pure coding without turning it into mush.

### DORA

Useful carry-forward ideas:

- software delivery is measured through capabilities and outcomes, not ideology
- AI changes performance dynamics but does not remove the need for good delivery systems
- stability and trust remain key

### MCP and agentic delivery

Useful carry-forward ideas:

- AI agents are now practical collaborators across the lifecycle
- MCP is a real standard for giving models reliable access to tools and context
- the industry is already moving toward terms like "agentic DevOps" and "AI-led SDLC"
- these phrases are useful as research anchors, but too generic to become the book's core branded method

## Waterfall / Predictive Project Management Position

The book should not caricature Waterfall.

Better framing:

- sequence itself is not the villain
- predictive planning is useful when uncertainty is low and cost of change is high
- the failure comes when certainty is overstated or context is frozen while reality moves

This lets the book sound adult instead of tribal.

## Methodology Design Constraints

The new methodology should:

- feel new without pretending history began yesterday
- carry the book, not just one chapter
- stay simple enough to explain to executives and practitioners
- be relevant beyond pure software coding
- retain familiar terms where they still work
- create new terms only where AI introduces genuinely new needs

## Conduit's Role

CONDUIT should not be the public methodology name.

Recommended role:

- one implementation of the broader method
- a case study
- a detailed example of how the method can be instantiated
- a source of concepts, but not the book's universal label

## Terminology Rules

### Keep familiar terms when continuity helps

Prefer to keep:

- backlog
- requirements
- acceptance criteria
- stakeholders
- governance
- architect
- service management
- release
- checkpoint if useful
- gate if useful
- CLI
- agent
- MCP

### Reframe familiar terms when AI changes the work

Candidates for reinterpretation:

- developer
- project manager
- business analyst
- QA
- Scrum Master
- Black Belt
- release manager

One useful argument to develop later:

> Many named roles in older methods solved recurring coordination problems. The label often changed more than the underlying need.

### Introduce genuinely new terms sparingly

Likely candidates:

- living spec
- audience impact
- autonomy modes

Even these should be challenged before adoption.

## Naming Direction

The methodology should likely be positioned as an **operating model**, but named with a single plain-language word in the style of Agile or Lean.

The best naming family is likely the ordinary adjective family rather than acronyms or compound enterprise phrases.

The methodology name should:

- be one word
- have low syllable count
- be easy to say aloud
- feel broad enough to grow
- not sound cute
- not require a glossary to survive first contact

## Revision Implications By Chapter

### Chapters 0-5

Keep mostly intact, but revise for consistency with the new thesis.

Key needs:

- foreshadow the bottleneck shift earlier
- reduce the sense that the book's destination is just methodology comparison
- sharpen the language around AI's effect on execution, judgment, and governance

### Chapter 5

This chapter likely needs a serious reframing, not because Agile becomes irrelevant, but because its continued relevance must now be argued differently.

### Chapters 6-12

These chapters become the proving ground for the new thesis.

They should explain:

- what still matters from existing methods
- what changes under AI
- what roles and workflows need reinterpretation
- how the new methodology works in practice

### Epilogue

The epilogue should eventually synthesize:

- old truths
- new bottleneck
- new operating model
- human agency in the age of AI

## Planned Assets

These are the first essential assets to support the pivot:

1. **Old Bottleneck vs New Bottleneck**
   - show the shift from code-production bottlenecks to context, coordination, governance, and integration bottlenecks

2. **Framework Map**
   - compare Lean, Agile, ITIL, PMBOK, BABOK, DORA, and the new methodology by problem solved, strength, and failure mode

3. **Work Lifecycle Map**
   - need -> intent -> analysis -> living spec -> build -> verify -> release -> operate -> learn

4. **Role Shift Map**
   - PM, BA, developer, QA, architect, service owner, AI agent

5. **Terminology Crosswalk**
   - familiar term / what stays / what shifts / what is genuinely new

6. **Conduit Example Flow**
   - only as a case-study-style example of implementation, not the universal book method

## Key Risks

### Risk 1: turning the book into a branded internal playbook

Mitigation:

- keep CONDUIT as an example, not the book's name
- avoid internal implementation detail in the main narrative

### Risk 2: overselling novelty

Mitigation:

- explicitly honor what is borrowed
- keep saying what remains true
- distinguish changed bottlenecks from changed principles

### Risk 3: too many new terms

Mitigation:

- retain familiar language whenever possible
- justify every new term by genuine conceptual necessity

### Risk 4: turning the book into software-only dogma

Mitigation:

- retain IT/software center of gravity
- broaden through PMBOK, BABOK, governance, stakeholder, and value-delivery language
- avoid pretending the same details apply equally everywhere

## Recommendation

Before revising Chapter 6 or any other late chapter, lock the book-level repositioning first.

That means:

1. choose the new argument
2. choose the naming direction
3. decide what legacy terms stay
4. map the second half of the book to the new spine
5. start the first assets around the bottleneck shift

Only then should chapter rewriting resume.
