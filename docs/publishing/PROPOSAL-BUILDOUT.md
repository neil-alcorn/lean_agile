# Proposal Buildout

This file is the structure for the nonfiction proposal we will assemble for *Lean and Agile*.

It is intentionally tailored to this project rather than copied from a generic template.

## 1. Working Title Block

- Title
- Subtitle
- Category
- Estimated word count
- Status of manuscript
- Status of proposal

### Current Draft Direction

- Title: *Lean and Agile*
- Subtitle direction: still open; should probably foreground leadership, AI, and enduring principles
- Category: business / leadership / organizational change / AI-at-work nonfiction

## 2. Proposal Overview

This should become a 2-3 page executive summary.

It needs to answer:

- what the book is
- who it is for
- why it matters now
- what makes it different
- why you are the right author

### What this book currently seems to promise

This book helps leaders and practitioners make sense of the family resemblance between Lean, Agile, Six Sigma, ITIL, Deming, and related improvement traditions, while showing how AI changes the tools but not the core truths of quality, trust, systems thinking, and leadership.

### Candid note

This promise is strong enough to keep building on, but still not sharp enough to submit.

The weak spot is not the idea. The weak spot is that the idea could still blur into "thoughtful general business book" unless we keep making it more concrete, more urgent, and more clearly yours.

## 3. Audience

We should define this in layers.

### Primary audience

- executives, directors, managers, and transformation leaders in medium-to-large organizations
- especially readers living inside IT-heavy or process-heavy environments
- readers trying to lead through AI change without losing trust, quality, or human agency

### Secondary audience

- project managers
- service managers
- coders and technical leads
- operations leaders
- consultants and trainers working in process improvement

### Audience language we should avoid

- "everyone"
- "all leaders"
- "all businesses"

## 4. Why Now

This section needs current urgency without trend-chasing.

The strongest current reasons appear to be:

- AI is intensifying the need for good systems, not replacing it
- organizations are overloaded with frameworks and jargon
- leaders need a humane operating philosophy for process, quality, and change
- readers are looking for practical clarity rather than methodology tribalism

## 5. Distinctive Angle

This is the section that keeps the book from sounding generic.

### Our best current angle

Most continuous-improvement frameworks are variations on recurring truths. The labels change. The human and operational realities do not.

### Additional differentiators

- humane, non-cynical voice
- AI included as a present reality, not a gimmick
- systems thinking tied to dignity, trust, and culture
- IT center of gravity with broad organizational relevance
- witty, readable tone instead of consultant-speak

### Pushback

We should be careful not to overrate "cross-framework clarity" by itself. That may matter to us more than it matters to a buyer.

The commercial version of the argument has to sound like a reader benefit, not a taxonomy cleanup project.

In other words:

- "These frameworks are related" is interesting.
- "This helps leaders make better decisions in the AI era without wrecking trust and quality" is stronger.

## 6. Comparable Titles

We should likely use 5 to 8 strong comps.

For each comp, capture:

- title
- author
- publisher
- year
- intended audience
- what it does well
- where our book differs

### Comp selection rules

- prefer recently published books
- include books the target reader would actually buy
- do not insult the comps
- show adjacency and distinction

## 7. Author Platform And Authority

This section will likely matter a lot.

We should inventory:

- leadership experience
- process and operational experience
- AI and workflow implementation experience
- speaking and training
- articles, posts, essays, workshops, courses, or presentations
- audience reach
- consulting credibility
- likely endorsement network

### Missing inputs to collect

- LinkedIn audience size
- newsletter or mailing list, if any
- speaking engagements in the last 24 months
- organizations that might buy in bulk
- podcast appearances or media mentions

## 8. Marketing / Publicity Plan

This should describe what you can really do, not what you vaguely hope to do.

### Likely components

- LinkedIn thought leadership
- talks / workshops / webinars
- professional communities and associations
- organizational or consulting network
- AI / operations / leadership audience crossover
- bulk purchases through teams or clients
- podcast outreach
- excerptable ideas for articles

## 9. Chapter Outline

We already have a chapter-level source tree. We should convert that into a proposal-facing chapter summary file later.

The chapter outline should show:

- the arc of the argument
- where AI enters the conversation
- where leadership and culture deepen the methodology discussion
- where visuals support learning

## 10. Sample Chapters

We should eventually choose 2 to 3 sample chapters.

Recommended candidates to consider once revised:

- Introduction
- Chapter 1
- Chapter 5 or Chapter 7

Reason:

- they show the conceptual frame
- they show practical application
- they give a sense of voice

### Warning

We should not choose sample chapters just because they are foundational.

We should choose the chapters that best prove:

- authority
- readability
- current relevance
- distinctiveness

If Chapter 1 is solid but Chapter 7 is sharper and more saleable, we use Chapter 7.

## 11. Supporting Materials

We should be ready to include or reference:

- chapter summaries
- visual philosophy
- sample diagrams/tables if they strengthen the proposal
- brief author bio
- contact sheet

## Build Order

Recommended drafting order:

1. audience

3. distinctive angle
4. comps
5. author platform

7. chapter outline
8. marketing plan
