# Chapter 2: Why Management Owns the Process but Employees Own the Results

## Opening Scenario: The Well-Intentioned Workflow

Picture this: Mike, an enthusiastic manager, strides into the weekly team meeting, practically buzzing with excitement. Over the past week, he’s crafted a dazzling new workflow: a masterpiece of flowcharts, timelines, and a spreadsheet tracker with more tabs than anyone thought humanly possible. To Mike, this isn’t just a workflow. It’s the key to unlocking the team’s potential and making everyone’s life easier.

But there’s a problem. Mike launches into an explanation of how the new workflow will work but glosses over the why behind it. As Simon Sinek famously said, “People don’t buy what you do; they buy why you do it.” Without understanding the purpose driving the change, the team listens politely but with blank expressions, unsure of how all the moving parts fit together or why this new process is even necessary.

At the end of the meeting, they nod, return to their desks, and then carry on as they always have. Over the next few weeks, productivity remains stagnant. Mike wonders why his meticulously designed process hasn’t transformed the team’s output.

## The Executive’s Role in Championing Change

The disconnect here is common in the corporate world: managers create the structure and employees take ownership of the results, but even well-intentioned teams with great workflows can fall flat without executive support. That support must begin before the workflow does by creating the conditions for cross-team collaboration. Even the best teams can’t succeed in a vacuum.

Senior leaders must explain why improvement is essential and how each role contributes to it. Otherwise, even the best efforts at the director or manager level can feel like band-aid solutions. Executives align people around the purpose of the change so managers and employees can collaborate meaningfully on processes and outcomes.

## The Dance Between Structure and Ownership

Continuous improvement depends on a partnership among executives, managers, and employees. Each plays a vital role, but the process begins at the top. Executives connect the need for change to the organization’s strategic goals. They also create the conditions for success by eliminating barriers, building trust, and investing in stable, long-term processes.

With that support, managers can design the frameworks, workflows, and guidelines that define the “how.” Employees bring those processes to life. This isn’t about issuing directives or setting rigid goals. It’s about making improvement a shared mission tied to innovation, quality, and customer satisfaction. When executives make improvement a priority, managers can design flexible processes and employees can own the results.

One practical way to think about this is simple enough to fit on an index card: map the process, measure the process, and manage the process. Employees cannot own results inside a process no one has made visible. Managers cannot improve what they cannot measure. Executives cannot demand accountability from a system they refuse to understand. The chain only works when all three levels do their part.

This is Chapter 1's correction made structural: quality is management's responsibility because management designs the process. But quality flourishes only when the employees inside that process feel empowered to adapt and improve it. Psychological safety, where employees can voice ideas and concerns without fear, is the backbone of this relationship. Executives set the direction, managers set the stage, and employees deliver the performance.

This doesn’t mean executives or managers should take a hands-off approach. They should create processes that allow for flexibility and adaptation. One way to stay engaged is through Gemba: going to where the work happens. By observing workflows and engaging with frontline employees, leaders can better understand challenges and provide meaningful support. When employees have no say in the work, results suffer. When they own the results, they bring creativity, insight, and pride to the process.

## Comparing Methodologies: Lean, Agile, and ITIL

Lean, Agile, and ITIL each balance process ownership and results ownership differently. Together, they offer practical ways to design processes that encourage employee ownership and adapt to real-world complexity.

This is also where leadership standard work matters. If executives and managers only engage the process when something is already on fire, employees learn that process is a punishment rather than a way of working. Ownership grows when priorities, review cadence, and support are consistent enough to trust.

**Lean:** Lean centers on reducing waste and ensuring that every step adds value. Executives establish Lean as an organizational priority, managers create standardized processes, and employees continually refine them. Tools such as value stream mapping, Kaizen events, and poka-yoke (mistake-proofing) help employees improve the work at each step. A manufacturing team member might stop the line to address an issue, or a developer might refactor code to remove an inefficiency. Managers provide the structure; the people doing the work provide the insight.

**Agile:** Agile gives teams ownership of results within a flexible framework. Its iterative model, often using sprints and incremental delivery, creates short feedback loops. Executives establish high-level goals while managers trust teams to self-organize, adapt, and iterate. Practices such as backlog prioritization, user stories, continuous integration, sprint planning, daily stand-ups, and retrospectives help teams refine both product and process. Teams are expected to learn quickly from small failures and use that knowledge to deliver better value.

**ITIL:** ITIL provides structure for managing IT services with stability and consistency. Executives set expectations for service quality, managers define practices for incident, problem, and change management, and employees troubleshoot within those boundaries. Service level agreements and incident-response workflows give employees room to make real-time decisions without sacrificing reliability. Root-cause analysis and risk assessment then help teams improve the service without abandoning necessary controls.

These methodologies differ in emphasis, but the division of responsibility is consistent: leadership champions improvement, management structures it, and the people closest to the work shape it.

## Why Letting Go Drives Better Results

Here’s where it gets interesting: It may seem logical for executives and managers to control both process and outcome, but this approach often backfires. When leaders try to micromanage every detail, they stifle creativity and engagement. Employees end up feeling like cogs in a machine rather than contributors to a larger mission.

Deming’s insight was that quality thrives when employees can adapt processes to the reality of the work. That can feel risky because relinquishing control requires trust. But when executives explain why improvement matters and managers create psychological safety instead of controlling every outcome, employees bring forward ideas, challenge norms, and innovate. Ownership improves the result and makes continuous improvement part of the culture.

In practice, executives might set customer satisfaction as a strategic goal, managers might provide adaptable processes that support it, and employees might make the daily improvements that turn the goal into reality. Leaders become facilitators rather than enforcers. Too often, managers dictate specific actions and monitor compliance, stealing ownership and pride from employees. Over time, people learn not to think and disengage from the mission.

## True Quality Comes from Empowerment

True quality emerges not from micromanaging outcomes but from empowering people to bring purpose and pride to their work. The framework should support rather than restrict. When employees own the results, they don’t merely execute a process. They shape and improve it.

For executives, this means championing change and communicating its importance. For managers, it means providing structures that guide without constraining. And for employees, it’s about owning the results and finding fulfillment in their work. In other words, quality isn’t enforced; it’s embraced.

## A Collaborative Shift

Let’s return to Mike and his team. Imagine he takes a different approach, with support from senior leadership. The executives have clarified why change is essential and empowered managers like Mike to bring their teams into the process. Instead of presenting his workflow as a finished product, Mike introduces it as a draft and offers to help employees meet the goals leadership has set. He doesn’t need to sell it as his idea or use it to advance his career. His job is to create a structure in which everyone can thrive, then invite the team to improve it. They discuss the purpose behind each step, identify potential roadblocks, and suggest practical adjustments Mike hadn’t considered. Together, they create a workflow that feels meaningful to the people who will use it.

In the weeks that follow, Mike sees a shift. The team uses the new workflow and adapts it as new challenges emerge. Productivity rises, and their investment in the results is visible. What started as Mike’s personal project has become a shared process that evolves with input from every level. Mike feels proud, not because he designed a perfect process, but because his team no longer needs him to have every answer.

## Reflection Questions

Balancing Structure and Ownership:

In your organization, do you see a clear distinction between the roles of management and employees when it comes to processes and results?

How could you help create a better balance where management provides structure while employees feel empowered to own the outcomes?

Micromanagement vs. Empowerment:

Have you ever experienced micromanagement, either as a leader or as an employee? What impact did it have on creativity and productivity?

What steps could you take to shift from micromanagement to a culture of trust and autonomy?

Psychological Safety and Ownership:

Does your current work environment encourage employees to take ownership of their roles without fear of failure?

What changes could you make to foster a safer space for employees to experiment, adapt, and learn?

Frameworks for Collaboration:

How effectively does your organization use frameworks like Lean, Agile, or ITIL to support collaboration between management and employees?

Are there opportunities to implement or improve these methodologies to strengthen teamwork and results?

Pride in Work:

Do employees in your organization feel pride in their work? How can you tell?

What specific actions could you take to cultivate a stronger sense of ownership and pride in your team or role?

Aligning Vision and Execution:

How well do you think your organization’s processes align with its broader vision and goals?

How could you help bridge any gaps between the strategic vision set by management and the daily execution driven by employees?

Reflection on Leadership:

As a leader, how do you support your team in owning their results while ensuring they have the structure and resources they need?

As an employee, do you feel equipped to deliver results within the systems your leadership has provided? What changes would help you feel more empowered?
